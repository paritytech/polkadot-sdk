**What was built** (each item spans interpreter + recompiler + linker + gas +
simulator + gastool + tests; `git show 3d2cab6` is the per-site checklist):

1. **Eight PVM instructions**, opcodes 232–239, `Latest64` only: `mul256`,
   `redc256`, `add256`, `sub256`, `mul256_by_u64`, and the fused
   `mul256_redc256`, `add256_redc256`, `sub256_redc256`.
2. **A curve25519-dalek fork with a `pvm64` backend** — 4×64 limbs instead of
   upstream's 5×51, intrinsics over the new instructions, ~1,000 field-element
   constants regenerated by a converter script, plus point-layer work. All
   `cfg`-gated; stock builds stay byte-identical.
3. **Two ISA semantics additions**, both spec-level rather than implementation
   detail: the **destroyed-register contract** (a wide op zeroes a fixed set
   plus its operands, which is what lets the recompiler use those host homes as
   save-free scratch with no analysis) and the **fault contract** (all operands
   probed before any guest state is touched, so faults report pristine
   registers and stay resume-safe under dynamic paging).
4. **A trampoline scheme in the recompiler** — the sequences run from 140 bytes
   (`add256`) to ~645 (`mul256_redc256`) against a 69-byte per-instruction
   budget, so they are emitted once per (opcode, operand-registers) and
   dispatched via a new vmctx continuation field that doubles as fault
   attribution. Any JAM implementation faces this sizing question.
5. **Determinism enforcement** — interpreter and recompiler held bit-identical
   on every register and every fault path by the step-tracing crosscheck,
   exemption-free.
6. **Tooling** — `tools/opcount` (per-opcode dynamic counts, gas, `--time` A/B),
   `build-wide-arith-blobs.sh` (3 routes × 5 benches, asserts controls stay
   byte-identical), benchtool wiring.
7. **Both reduction routes**, so whether `redc256` earns a permanent opcode is a
   measured question rather than a taste one: it does — dropping it costs +32%
   gas, and +64% wall in-container (93.6 vs 57.2 µs, never re-measured on the
   rig because the gas delta alone settled it).

## Scope

Eight PVM instructions — `mul256`, `add256`, `sub256`, `mul256_by_u64`,
`redc256`, and the fused `mul256_redc256`, `add256_redc256`, `sub256_redc256` —
implemented across interpreter, amd64 recompiler and linker, plus a 4×64-limb
curve25519-dalek backend to exercise them. The workload is signature
verification: ed25519 (two implementations), sr25519, with ECDSA benches as
controls.

The problem being solved: PVM inherits RISC-V's lack of wide-arithmetic
primitives, and its recompiler translates 1:1, so 256-bit field arithmetic
becomes long limb loops with carry emulation and heavy spilling. These
instructions take whole limb loops into one opcode operating on memory operands.

**Both reduction routes are implemented and measured** — `redc256` versus
generic `mul256_by_u64` glue — because `redc256` is the one family member
encoding a number-theoretic idiom, and whether it earns a permanent opcode was
left to the measurement (see "The redc256 question").

Background, not needed to read this doc:
[wide-arith-handoff.md](wide-arith-handoff.md) (build order and plumbing) and
[wide-arith-instr-research.md](wide-arith-instr-research.md) (instruction
semantics and the pre-implementation analysis). This work is the second in a
series: its predecessor fused the `mulhu`+`mul` pair into a single widening
multiply ([mul-wide-results.md](mul-wide-results.md), −9…−11% wall), and its
successor would be the batch family (`mul256x4`).

## Where the numbers come from

Per-opcode dynamic counts (tools/opcount, new step-tracing profiler):

- **Wide-op counts per verify** (redc route): mul256 = 2,976
  (= 1,445 fe_mul + 1,531 fe_square), redc256 = 2,976; sub256 = 3,087
  (3 per fe_sub: op + two borrow folds); add256 = 3,372; no-redc variant:
  12,300 add256 + 2,976 mul256_by_u64 (1 mul + 3 folds per reduction).
- Getting from the first-cut backend (191k) to 88.9k took two passes, both
  instructive for any "hand-written backend" effort:
  1. **Field-layer copies (191k → 133k):** in-place pointer intrinsics (the
     read-all-then-write/aliasing-tolerant spec is what makes this safe),
     write-once outputs so LLVM merges the alloca with the caller's variable,
     a static fold-constant table, `#[inline(always)]` throughout.
  2. **Point-layer memcpys (133k → 88.9k):** `compiler_builtins::memcpy` was
     50,433 instr/verify (~430 calls) — 96–160-byte point-struct copies in
     the inlined Straus/NAF loop that LLVM won't inline on riscv. Fixed
     (gated on the pvm backend cfg, stock builds byte-identical) by
     reference-returning vartime table selects, in-place point additions in
     the double-base loop, and field-wise `CompletedPoint` writes. memcpy is
     now 5,821/26 calls (sha512 staging + setup).
- Remaining composition (redc, per-function): scalar-mul loop + adds ≈ 42.6k,
  point double 26.8k, memcpy 5.8k, sha512 3.8k, ct_eq 3.8k, decompress 3.0k,
  Scalar52 1.1k. Residual per-wide-op glue is ~2 `add_imm_64` of address
  materialization — inherent to a memory-operand ISA.

Calibrated gas, computed from the counts but **not implemented** (naive
pricing of 1/instruction is what ships): with plausible per-op costs of
mul256=16, redc256=8, add/sub256=4, mul256_by_u64=4, the redc variant comes to
≈218k gas-cycles. So **naive pricing undercharges these instructions by ~1.6×**
on this workload — a cost-constants decision, not a design change.

First measurement — **before** the recompiler optimization iteration:

| benchmark | host portable | host native | PVM redc | PVM baseline (same rig) | Δ wall |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| ed25519-zebra | 24.48 µs | 23.05 µs | 62.62 µs | 57.24 µs | +9.4% |
| ed25519 | 29.21 µs | 24.34 µs | 65.20 µs | 59.81 µs | +9.0% |
| sr25519 | 24.94 µs | 26.51 µs | 62.37 µs | 56.68 µs | +10.0% |
| ecdsa-k256 (control) | 61.46 µs | 59.60 µs | 166.31 µs | 166.58 µs | flat ✓ |
| ecdsa-libsecp (control) | 112.40 µs | 116.23 µs | 161.56 µs | 162.54 µs | flat ✓ |

Diagnosis: gas counts PVM instructions, the CPU executes recompiled x86 —
and the V1 sequences carried ~45–55 x86 of per-op overhead (register
save/restores, operand probes, trampoline dispatch), so total executed x86
stayed ≈ 1.0M ≈ baseline. Optimization iterations so far (second bench run:
60.0 µs, recovered 2.6 of the 5.4 µs regression):

1. call/ret trampoline dispatch (the return was an indirect jump with
   alternating targets — a chronic mispredict at ~12.4k ops/verify);
2. point-layer memcpy elimination in the guest (50k → 5.8k instr/verify);
3. **fused mul256+redc256 trampolines**: the dalek backend emits both
   instructions from one asm! block (157/157 pairs adjacent), and the
   recompiler folds the product straight from host registers — one overhead
   block instead of two and a store→load round trip removed from the serial
   field dependency chain, ~2,976 times per verify. Gas is charged per
   instruction as before (metering unchanged; redc blob is 89,743 after the
   single-asm-block change, +0.9%).

4. **the store-to-load forwarding stall (the big one)**: the destination
   probe was a 1-byte read-modify-write; its byte store plus the full-width
   result store were later partially overlapped by the next field op's
   8-byte load — multi-store overlap defeats forwarding (~20 cycles), on
   every edge of the serial field dependency chain. This stall is why the
   instruction-count reductions (1–3) barely moved the wall clock: the
   workload was stall-bound, not throughput-bound. Widening the probes to
   8 bytes (identical fault-contract guarantees) fixed it.
5. **liveness-based scratch stealing** (after the stall fix made the
   workload throughput-bound again, so instruction counts pay off): a
   bounded, block-local backward scan finds guest registers that are dead at
   each wide-op site (written before ever being read further down the
   block); their host homes become save-free scratch for the kernels,
   eliminating 2–5 of the 8–12 push/pop pairs per operation. Disabled under
   step tracing; fault-safe for free because all faults happen in the probe
   phase, before any clobbering. This is also the one optimization that
   relaxes an observable (dead registers' values at execution end — see the
   spec note under the final table). A follow-up experiment showed the
   remaining saves are near-irreducible: the live registers hold the field
   buffers' addresses, and neither clobber-declarations in the guest nor
   hand-written asm can shrink that working set (see the negative-results
   section).

## The destroyed-register contract (clobbers as ISA semantics) — measured

After the liveness scan was rejected (recompiler must stay a 1:1 mapper —
Jan's ruling) and its commits reverted, the same win was re-earned the
Jan-endorsed way: **the destroyed set is part of the instruction's
semantics**, not an analysis result. Every wide-arithmetic instruction now
architecturally *destroys* a fixed register set plus its operand registers —
they read as **zero** after the instruction retires (deterministic, not
"unspecified"); the carry-out is written after the zeroing; a faulting
instruction destroys nothing. The mul family destroys {T0–T2, A0–A5} (9);
add256/sub256 destroy {T0–T2, A0–A3} (7, sparing A4/A5 between multiplies).
RA/SP/S0/S1 are never in a fixed set (s0/s1 are not legal rv64e asm
clobbers; destroying RA would break `op; ret`).

Consequences, all implemented on `mku-wide-arith`:

- **Recompiler**: destroyed homes are save-free scratch — no analysis, a
  fixed per-opcode rule. The fused kernel drops from 12 push/pop pairs to 3,
  mul256 to 2, add/sub to 2; each sequence ends in ~7–9 `xor` zeroing idioms
  (rename-eliminated, no memory traffic).
- **Interpreter zeroes the identical set** — the step-tracing crosscheck now
  covers *every* register on *every* path (the liveness-era "dead registers
  exempt from crosscheck" carve-out is gone, and with it the spec question
  about unspecified register state).
- **The fused pair had to become a real opcode** (`mul256_redc256` = 237,
  operands m_d/m_s1/m_s2/r_d, fold constant hardcoded in **A4**): a
  standalone mul256 now destroys the registers an adjacent redc256 would
  read, so a recompiler peephole (or any implicit fusion) is incoherent —
  under per-instruction stepping the pair *must* execute unfused, which the
  contract breaks. The guest emits the canonical two-word pair in one asm!
  block; the linker collapses it at decode time (k pinned to A4 is the
  fusion precondition). This also resolves the documented fused-path fault
  divergence: interpreter and recompiler now probe in the same order by
  construction.
- **Guest**: intrinsics pin operands to fixed registers inside the destroyed
  set and declare the rest as clobbers — rustc, the one party that knows
  liveness, arranges the code around them (Jan's memset pattern).

Measured cost/benefit (in-container, generic sandbox, core-pinned
interleaved A/B, old-binary+old-blobs vs new-binary+new-blobs; the relative
delta is the meaningful number):

| bench (redc set) | old | new | Δ wall | gas (naive) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| ed25519-zebra | 63.0 µs | 57.2 µs | **−9.2%** | 89,743 → 109,685 |
| ed25519 | 66.3 µs | 60.5 µs | **−8.8%** | 86,534 → ~106k |
| sr25519 | 63.8 µs | 59.0 µs | **−7.5%** | 105,749 → ~126k |
| noredc zebra | 94.4 µs | 93.6 µs | −0.8% | 117,161 → 175,684 |

The gas increase (+22% redc) is the guest-side spill/re-materialization glue

## Source-probe removal via a vmctx save area (optimization 1): container-negative, rig-positive

Measured, not modelled, on a knob-gated measurement build
(branch `mku-wide-arith-probe-experiment`, off `04bfc06`) before building any
fault-path machinery — the probe saving and the price of making a mid-body
fault recoverable were priced *separately*, per opcode family.

**Verdict: adopt for `add256`/`sub256` only.** On machine B the design is worth
**−1.2…−1.7 µs/verify** (drift-corrected; −1.81…−2.29 raw), carried entirely by
the add/sub family; the fused opcode loses ~0.6–1.0 µs and `redc256` is
neutral. This *reverses* the in-container reading below, which had the design as
a +0.2…+2.4 µs regression, and it does so for the reason the container is
already known to distort: it over-weights save/restore memory traffic, which is
all mode C adds. The distortion is much larger than the ~0.7× seen on the
contract experiment — mode C costs +2.8…+3.6 µs in the container and
**+0.1…+0.6 µs (inside the error bar) on the rig**.

**Methodology finding, first-class:** for anything whose cost *is* save/restore
traffic, the container is not a usable proxy — not even for the sign. In-container
A/B remains reliable for instruction-count and stall effects (it found the
store-forwarding stall), but a store-traffic trade has to be settled on the rig.
Partial adoption is available for free because probes and saves are emitted per
trampoline, so the per-family rows are the actionable output, not the aggregate.

Two orthogonal, family-scoped knobs
(`POLKAVM_WIDE_ARITH_EXPERIMENT_NO_SRC_PROBES`, `_VMCTX_SAVES`, each
`all|mul,redc,addsub,mulu64,fused`) give four modes: **A** default, **B**
source probes dropped and nothing paid in return (fault-incorrect — the upper
bound), **C** every probe kept but the operand snapshots and non-destroyed
scratch saves moved from the stack into a `VmCtx::wide_arith_save[8]` area
(fault-*correct*, so the full suite validates the plumbing), **D** = B + C,
i.e. the shippable design's hot path. One binary, one blob set, mode entirely
env-driven — no worktree, no blob rebuild, no pre/post-contract pairing
hazard; gas is unchanged in every mode (109,685 zebra) which doubles as the
stale-blob check.

Two findings, with their scope corrected by the rig run:

1. **A per-operation cost model derived in-container does not port.** The
   container reading (mode C costing the fused kernel ~1.6 cycles/op for 8
   slots but `redc256` ~8 cycles/op for 2–3, i.e. "kernel slack, not slot
   count, sets the price") described the container's inflated store traffic;
   on the rig mode C is inside the error bar everywhere. What survives is the
   *ranking*, not the coefficients.
2. **Removing `redc256`'s source probe alone is neutral on both machines**
   (+0.34 µs in-container, −0.36…−0.44 µs on the rig, both inside their error
   bars). Its source is 64 bytes and the probe touches the first and last 8 —
   plausibly acting as a prefetch ahead of the dependent `mulx` stream. Still a
   hypothesis, but it does argue against assuming probe removal is
   monotonically good.

Corroboration for the rig numbers: the mode-A baseline (48.76 µs zebra) lands
within 3.5% of the 47.11 µs of record despite a different sandbox and harness,
which is the check that this is the same machine. An earlier bare-metal attempt
with `--linux` on an unquiesced box produced a +4.17 µs delta on the *control* —
larger than every real effect in its own table — and is discarded; it also
carried ~+45 µs/iteration of zygote round-trip, diluting every percentage. The
generic sandbox is the right harness for this comparison, the runner now derives
the error bar from the control before printing anything, and it alternates mode
order per round.

What is *not* closed: with add/sub adopted, the **remaining** unclaimed share is
the fused and `redc256` families — roughly 1.2–1.5 µs of the rig's 2.4–2.7 µs
total probe cost. Reaching that still needs a design paying **nothing** per
operation: no saves at all, with a faulting wide-arithmetic instruction zeroing
its *entire* destroyed set including operand registers, which makes a source
fault non-resumable (the host gets a `Trap` where it now gets a resumable
`Segfault`). Trading a dynamic-paging capability for ~1.2–1.5 µs (~2.5%) is a
materially weaker case than it looked before the rig run, when the same
amendment appeared to be the only route to the whole 3 µs. Cheaper and
spec-free, worth measuring first: make the probes *shorter* rather than absent —
each source probe is three instructions (`mov r32, ptr`, two `cmp [mem], 0` at
offsets 0 and length−8), and one discarded 32-byte vector load over the operand
touches the same bytes and faults on the same pages, cutting three to two with
the fault contract intact. It needs a vector load in polkavm-assembler and
re-verification of fault-order equivalence against the OOB/dynamic-paging tests;
the old xmm negative result does not apply (that measured GPR↔vector
*transfers*, this is a load whose result is never read). Note
`llvm-mca` cannot pre-screen any of this: its znver3 model charges every
`adc`/`adcx`/`adox` as occupying all four ALU pipes (96 cyc/op for the fused
kernel vs 53.8 measured), so it would report the added stores as free.

## Fused add/sub folds (optimization 2): −18% on machine B — NUMBERS OF RECORD

Two new opcodes, `add256_redc256` (238) and `sub256_redc256` (239):
`[d] = ([s1] ± [s2])` with the carry/borrow-out folded back in modulo
`2^256 - k`. They replace `fe_add`'s `add256` + carry-store + `redc256` and
`fe_sub`'s three-`sub256` chain with one instruction each.

polkavm `mku-wide-arith` `aa0dff7`; guest `curve25519-4.1.3--dev` `ea42305`
(fe_add) and `26e6ba4` (fe_sub); blob set
`wide-arith-blobs-fusedfold/`.

Design points worth carrying:

- **Two folds always suffice, and neither is a multiply.** The carry/borrow is
  0 or 1, so `k·carry` is a `cmov`, not a `mulx` — no `rdx`, and the kernel
  needs exactly the scratch `add256` already needed (5 registers), so the
  family keeps the smaller `ADD_SUB` destroyed set and `A4`/`A5` stay spared
  for the guest compiler. A third fold would need `2k > 2^256`; `k < 2^64`.
- **`add256_redc256` is bit-identical to the pair it replaces**, being defined
  as `redc256` applied to the exact 257-bit sum. No result changes anywhere.
- **`k` is an explicit operand** (no `A4` pinning). See the k-convention note
  in "Implementation notes" below.

## Clobber declarations: the hint version failed, the contract version worked

These are two implementations of one idea — *let the guest tell the VM which
registers a wide operation may destroy, so the recompiler can skip saving
them*. They got opposite results. The distinction is recorded here because the
first result reads like evidence against the second, and it is not.

**Version 1 — clobbers as a hint (falsified, reverted).** The guest
over-declares clobbers on its inline-asm blocks; the recompiler runs a
block-local liveness scan and uses as save-free scratch only the registers it
can *prove* are dead at that site.

- The provably-dead count per wide-op site stayed at **~4.2 regardless of how
  many the guest declared.** LLVM's answer to over-declaring is to spill the
  field-buffer addresses into *other* registers — and those are live at the next
  site, so nothing is actually freed.
- The guest paid **+4–8% gas** for that, in extra spill/reload glue, and got no
  reduction in save/restore work.

**Version 2 — clobbers as a contract (shipped).** The ISA *specifies* the
destroyed set, so nothing has to be proven: **9 registers** for the mul family,
unconditionally, on every site.

- **−5.4…−6.4% wall on machine B** (−8…−9% in-container; the container
  overweights save/restore traffic).
- The remaining cost is the guest glue that keeps live values out of the
  destroyed set: **+22% gas** (89,743 → 109,685, spill/re-materialization of
  pointers the instructions now destroy). That is the trade the contract makes —
  more guest instructions, fewer host save/restores — and on wall clock it wins.
  Under the priority in force (wall only, gas is free) it is the right side of
  the trade; a gas-constrained caller would judge it differently.

**The point:** the old conclusion *"~4 dead registers per site is the ceiling"*
was a property of **requiring proof**, not a property of the workload. A
liveness scan can never see what a specification can simply assert. (The scan
was separately vetoed on design grounds — an observable-state change gated on an
analysis the spec cannot pin down — and reverted in `4262d03`. So the contract
is not merely the better-performing option; it is the only one of the two that
was ever admissible.)

## How the instructions are implemented (recompiler anatomy)

For readers who haven't lived in `compiler/amd64.rs`: what one of these
instructions actually turns into, how heavy it is, and why the set has eight
members.

### Site stub + shared trampoline

A normal PVM instruction compiles to a few bytes of inline x86. The wide
instructions compile to hundreds (table below) — far over the recompiler's
per-instruction worst-case size budget (~69 B), which the code-layout
machinery and the adversarial-bloat bound depend on. So each (opcode,
operand-register combination) is emitted **once**, as a shared *trampoline*
at the end of the module's native code (the same mechanism upstream already
uses for `memset` — this is a storage decision, not analysis; the mapping
stays 1:1 and context-free), and each guest instruction becomes a ~25-byte
stub: store the continuation address into a vmctx field (fault attribution —
identifies *which* site is inside the shared code), then `call` the
trampoline. `call`/`ret` keeps the return branch predicted by the return
stack buffer even with thousands of sites sharing one trampoline.

### The trampoline pipeline (phases, in order)

1. **Probes** — touch the first and last 8 bytes of every memory operand
   (reads for sources, read-modify-write for destinations; sources first,
   destinations last), using only the non-guest TMP register (`rcx`).
   **Every possible fault happens here**, with all guest state pristine —
   this is what makes faults deterministic and dynamic-paging restarts safe.
   8-byte (not 1-byte) touches: narrower probe stores defeat
   store-to-load forwarding on the field dependency chain (~20 cycles/edge,
   measured — iteration 4).
2. **Operand snapshots** — operand register *values* pushed to the native
   stack where the body needs them after clobbering their homes (the
   5-operand fused multiply snapshots all five).
3. **Saves** — push the 2–3 scratch registers *outside* the destroyed set
   (homes of RA/SP/S0/S1). Destroyed-set homes are used as scratch with no
   save — that is the contract's entire point.
4. **Kernel** — loads, arithmetic, all fault-free (everything was probed).
5. **Stores** — results written; ordered after all loads (aliasing safety).
6. **Restores** — pop the saves from phase 3.
7. **Zeroing** — `xor` every destroyed register (fixed set ∪ operands);
   the carry-out, where the op has one, is written *after* this.
8. `ret` to the site stub.

### The exact register contract

| | operands | fixed destroyed set | zeroed on retirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| `mul256`, `redc256`, `mul256_by_u64`, `mul256_redc256` | 3–4 regs (+k in A4 for 237) | {T0,T1,T2,A0..A5} | fixed ∪ operands |
| `add256`, `sub256`, `add256_redc256`, `sub256_redc256` | 4 regs | {T0,T1,T2,A0..A3} | fixed ∪ operands |

- Never destroyed: **RA, SP, S0, S1** (s0/s1 aren't legal rv64e asm
  clobbers; destroying RA breaks `op; ret`) — plus A4/A5 for the add/sub
  family, deliberately left alive between multiplies for the guest compiler.
  This is a constraint, not a choice: the entire pool available to a guest
  inline-asm block as operands or clobbers is `{ra, t0–t2, a0–a5}`, which is
  what caps a fixed destroyed set at 9 registers.
- Carry/borrow-out: written **after** the zeroing, so it may alias any
  operand.
- A **faulting** instruction destroys nothing — all faults precede all
  clobbers (phase 1).
- Host side: `rcx` is the always-free TMP; the 13 guest registers map 1:1
  onto host registers (`regmap.rs`); the guest's asm! declares the destroyed
  set as clobbers, so LLVM — not the VM — keeps live values out of it.

### How heavy these ops are (x86 per instruction, post-contract)

Trampoline bodies are straight-line (no loops), so static ≈ executed:

| opcode | trampoline size | ≈ x86 instr | ×/verify (zebra, redc route) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `mul256_redc256` | 645 B (measured) | ~160 | 2,976 |
| `mul256` (standalone) | ~470 B (measured) | ~120 | 0 |
| `redc256` (standalone) | 236 B (measured) | ~60 | 0 |
| `add256` / `sub256` | 140 B (measured) | ~43 | 72 / 0 |
| `add256_redc256` / `sub256_redc256` | ~170 B (static count) | ~51 | 1,100 / 1,029 |

So one PVM "instruction" here is 40–160 x86 instructions — of which the
kernel proper is roughly 60–70% and the rest is the fixed overhead
(probes ~9, snapshots/saves, zeroing ~7–9, dispatch). The fused multiply
kernel retires in **53.8 cycles/op measured** (~3 IPC — the mulx +
adcx/adox dual carry chain pipelines well; note `llvm-mca`'s znver3 model
mis-prices it at 96 cycles by charging every `adc*` as occupying all four
ALU pipes — do not use it to pre-screen these kernels). Per verify, the
~6,200 wide ops expand to roughly 500–600k x86 instructions — the density
is why every per-op overhead instruction shows up multiplied by thousands
on the wall clock, and why the optimization history is dominated by
overhead trimming.

### Why eight opcodes

Five **generic** ops (`mul256`, `add256`, `sub256`, `mul256_by_u64`,
`redc256`) — the composable 256-bit arithmetic any bignum user (EVM/resolc,
secp256k1) can build on — plus three **fused** ops that add no new
semantics (each is definitionally the composition of generic ops) but pay
the fixed per-op overhead once instead of twice or three times:

- `redc256` is the one *number-theoretic* opcode and was contested from the
  start; the measured defense: the generic route without it costs **+32%
  gas and +64% wall** (the (d)-vs-(e) experiment). It serves any modulus of
  the form 2^256−k with k < 2^64 (25519's 38, secp256k1's 2^32+977).
- The fused ops exist because the destroyed-register contract makes
  registers not survive across wide ops — so multi-op idioms *cannot* be
  recompiler-level peephole fusions (incoherent under per-instruction
  stepping) and must be architectural instructions or nothing. Their
  measured worth: the multiply pair ≈ 2 µs; the add/sub folds −18%
  wall and −16% gas (most of which is the *guest glue* the fusion deletes:
  scratch-buffer zeroing stores and fold-table indexing — see the
  instruction-mix table above).

## SIMD/vector kernels for mul256 — measured and analyzed, rejected for single ops

The register-pressure story invites "just use the vector units"; it was
explored in three distinct forms. Epistemic status is marked per item —
one is measured, one is analysis, one is the surviving open path.

1. **xmm as save space (measured, dead).** Replacing the kernels' stack
   push/pops with `movq` to/from xmm registers — zero memory traffic, and
   the vector file is dead space under PVM. Regressed **7–10 µs/verify**:
   a GPR↔vector domain crossing costs ~3–6 cycles per `movq`, ×~24 per
   operation, with the restore chain on the critical path. This killed the
   whole class of "stash GPR state in vector registers" ideas
   (`POLKAVM_WIDE_ARITH_XMM_SAVES` knob, commit `d905edf`).
2. **A single-op AVX-512/IFMA mul256 kernel (analyzed, not measured —
   the dev container is Zen 3, no AVX-512).** Three independent blockers:
   - *Radix conversion is architecturally mandatory, both directions.*
     IFMA computes in radix-2^52 lanes; the ISA speaks canonical 4×64
     limbs in memory — and `mul256`'s spec requires the full 512-bit
     product stored in canonical form, so the kernel cannot stay in
     radix-52 internally: it must fully carry-normalize and repack
     (~15–20 lane-serial ops, the classic IFMA pain point), plus ~6–10
     ops per operand on the way in. The GPR kernel gets canonical form
     for free because it computes in it. This blocker is structural.
   - *Zen 4 double-pumps AVX-512*: every 512-bit op is 2×256-bit µops —
     a ~90–95-op zmm kernel is ~180 µops against the GPR body's ~150
     cheap ops. On true-512 hardware (Intel, Zen 5) this flips to
     interesting; on the machines at hand it doesn't.
   - *No latency hiding for a lone product*: one multiplication is a
     serial chain of 4-cycle `vpmadd52` steps plus conversion chains fore
     and aft, against 16 `mulx` pipelining at 1/cycle (~20–25 cycles
     total, 53.8 cycles/op measured for the whole fused kernel).
3. **What survives: batching**, as a separate piece of work. All three
   blockers amortize or vanish when four independent products run in lanes:
   conversions divide
   by 4 (or disappear if lanes stay resident across a region), the lanes
   fill with independent work so latencies pipeline, and — key point from
   the xmm experiment — **memory→vector loads do not pay the domain
   crossing** (that penalty is register-file transit specifically). The
   design rule for a future `mul256x4`: operands enter the vector file
   from memory, stay resident across the batched region, and never
   transit GPRs. This is a separate ISA discussion (batch-shaped field
   code in the guest), not an extension of the current scalar set. RVV
   (the guest-side vector story) was raised with Jan as its own large
   topic and is out of scope here.

## Implementation notes that affect the spec (a GP proposal must carry these)

1. **Native code size forces a trampoline scheme.** The recompiled sequences
   are hundreds of bytes (mul256 ≈ 470B), far over polkavm's 69-byte
   per-instruction worst-case budget. Sequences are emitted once per
   (opcode, operand-registers) as shared trampolines; each site is a ~25-byte
   stub (store continuation → new vmctx field, jump). This also bounds
   adversarial code bloat: worst case is the operand-encoding space
   (~90k variants), not code size. Any JAM implementation faces the same
   sizing question.
2. **Fault contract:** every trampoline
   probes the first+last 8 bytes of each memory operand (read-probes for
   sources, 8-byte RMW for dst; sources first, dst last) using only the
   non-guest TMP register, *before* touching any guest state or the stack.
   All faults therefore report pristine guest registers and are resume-safe
   under dynamic paging; the fault-free body then runs to completion. The
   interpreter mirrors this (T0 saved/restored scratch + dst write-probe
   pass), giving exact interpreter/compiler equivalence on every fault path —
   verified by the crosscheck suite incl. OOB, aliasing, page-boundary and
   register-sweep tests.
3. **No address wrap-around:** pointers are truncated to 32 bits; limb `i`
   lives at `(ptr mod 2^32) + 8i` with no wrap — operands extending past
   4 GiB trap (matches the pre-existing behaviour of an 8-byte access at
   0xFFFFFFFF).
4. **The fold constant `k` is passed two different ways, deliberately.** A
   reviewer will ask, so: `mul256_redc256` (237) has *five* operands (three mul
   pointers, the redc destination, and `k`) for four codec slots, so its `k` is
   hardcoded to `A4`. `add256_redc256`/`sub256_redc256` (238/239) have four for
   four, so `k` is an explicit register operand — which also keeps it out of
   the destroyed-set special cases (the operand union covers it) and lets the
   guest compiler park the constant wherever it likes. The asymmetry is in the
   operand counts, not in the design intent — and it is removable: 237's fifth
   operand exists only because the fused pair architecturally writes the
   512-bit product, which the guest never reads, so dropping that write frees
   the slot and makes `k` the fourth register operand everywhere (backlog item
   1 in the probe-removal hand-off, worth ~24k stores/verify on its own). A GP
   should either land that first or carry the operand-count rationale
   explicitly; it should not present the two conventions as unexplained.
   Guest encodings: `.insn r4 0xb,6,{0,1}` = add/sub fold, `rd` = `k`,
   `rs1`/`rs2` = sources, `rs3` = dst pointer. This needs its own `func3`
   because the R4 group's `func2` selector is 2 bits wide and 3 of its 4 values
   were already taken — worth knowing before anyone adds a sixth wide shape.
   The zero register is rejected for `k`, so a guest wanting `k = 0` (legal;
   it degenerates to `mod 2^256`) must materialize the zero itself.
5. **These sequences are heavier than a limb-loop count suggests**: mul256 ≈ 112
   executed x86 instructions, redc256 ≈ 70, add256 ≈ 40 — roughly 1.3–2.9× the
   arithmetic alone. The overhead is operand probes (~6–9) and scratch
   save/restores (~14–24; read-all-then-write needs the full 512-bit product in
   registers, and 13 of 16 host registers hold guest state). Both were then
   attacked directly: the destroyed-register contract removed most of the
   save/restores, and fusion removed whole probe sets. Anyone pricing a wide
   instruction from its arithmetic alone will underestimate it by this much.
